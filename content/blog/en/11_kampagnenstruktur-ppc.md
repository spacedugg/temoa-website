# Amazon campaign structure: a PPC architecture that actually scales

Most Amazon advertising accounts do not grow, they sprawl. New campaigns get stacked on old ones reactively, ad groups fill up with keywords thrown together, match types blur into each other, and after eighteen months the account looks like a filing cabinet nobody has tidied since launch. The data is there but unreadable. And what you cannot read, you cannot optimize, let alone scale. This is exactly the wall many sellers hit between their first and second million in revenue.

## Why structure matters more the bigger you get

With three products and two campaigns, even chaos works. With fifty ASINs and a six-figure ad budget, the structure decides whether you can still steer the account at all. A clear architecture makes sure every campaign has one unambiguous job, that you can direct budget deliberately and that your reporting means something. Structure is not there for its own sake, it is the precondition for any sensible optimization.

## The basic principle: separate by function

A scalable structure separates campaigns by their function, not by accident. Three roles are fundamental:

- **Discovery:** auto campaigns and broad keywords that find out which search terms convert. This is where you look for new profitable keywords.
- **Performance (the harvest):** exactly targeted campaigns for the keywords you already know sell. This is where you deliberately put more budget and higher bids.
- **Defense and brand:** campaigns on your own brand keywords, to keep competitors away from your buyers.

This separation is the backbone. It stops the same keywords bidding against each other across several campaigns.

## The keyword funnel principle

The engine of a good structure is a loop: in the discovery campaigns you find out from the search term report which searches convert. You move those winners into an exact performance campaign, and add them as negative keywords in the discovery campaign. That way every keyword travels from searching to harvesting, and your campaigns do not cannibalize each other. This funnel is what keeps an account clean and steerable over time.

## One campaign, one goal

A common mistake is filling a single campaign with twenty different keywords and every match type. The result: you cannot direct budget precisely, because a good and a bad keyword sit in the same pot. Tighter granularity works better, with few closely related keywords per ad group and one clear match type purpose per campaign. That way you can raise bids and budgets where it pays off, without co-financing losses somewhere else.

## A consistent naming logic

It sounds trivial and it is decisive in large accounts: one naming scheme throughout. If every campaign follows the same pattern (for instance *product line | ad type | function | match type*), you can filter, sort and understand your account at a glance. With no system, thirty campaigns are already too many to keep track of. With one, even an account with hundreds of campaigns stays readable.

## Rebuild cleanly or restructure?

If your account is already a mess, the question is: tidy up or rebuild? A complete rebuild is risky because you lose ranking history. A step-by-step restructure is usually better: first move the top performers into a clean structure, then set up discovery properly, then bring the rest across. Do not change everything at once but in controlled stages, so you can judge the effect of each change.

## In short

Scaling rarely fails on budget and often on structure. Whoever separates campaigns clearly by function, establishes a keyword funnel, keeps granularity high and uses a consistent naming scheme builds an account that can be read and steered, which is the basic requirement for growing past the million mark. A tidy architecture is boring, and it is the difference between an account you control and one that controls you.
